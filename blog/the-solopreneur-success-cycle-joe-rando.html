The Solopreneur Success Cycle: How to Build a Sustainable One-Person Business

April 10, 202526 minInterview
Listen on Spotify Apple Podcasts Watch on YouTube

Joe Rando built a framework called the Solopreneur Success Cycle. It gives a one-person business a way to grow without burning out. The cycle starts with one honest question about your life, then loops through action and learning so the business keeps serving you. Joe joined me on The Undervalued Expert to walk through it.

Here's the short version. If you chose to run a solo business, you didn't choose to scale at any cost. Employees are the strongest scaling tool there is, and you gave them up on purpose. So there's another reason you're doing this. Joe calls that reason your goals. Build the business around those goals, and you build something you want to keep running.

Start with your life goals, not the growth chart

Joe learned this the hard way. He once turned a software company into a service company to keep it alive. He hated running it. That pain changed how he thinks. When you start, run, or reshape a business, your life goals belong front and center. He named his platform LifeStarr for that reason. The business should serve your life.

Your goals can be about income. They can also be about dinner with your family every night, or skiing when the snow is good, or doing work you're proud of. Joe's point is simple. Figure out what a good life looks like before you design the business that pays for it. A consultant who wants family dinners every night will build a different business than one who travels to client sites. Same skill, different shape.

The Solopreneur Success Cycle, step by step

Joe calls it seven steps, with a step zero that most people skip. Step zero is your goals and your definition of success. After that you envision the business. You picture what you could do and how it might look. Then you plan it. Then you set it up. Then you execute.

Execution isn't the end. You execute and learn at the same time. You watch what works, what fails your goals, and what you got wrong about the plan. Then you refine or reimagine the business. You decide which changes matter most. You make the adjustment, and you loop back to execute again. It's a flywheel that keeps the business in step with your life and with a changing world. Plenty of solo owners have had to spin that wheel since AI arrived.

Execute and learn at the same time

Most first plans break. You build a service you love, email the people who should want it, and hear crickets. That's normal. The owners who cling to the first plan are the ones who stall. Joe treats every launch as a draft. He ships, watches the real world react, and adjusts.

How often should you step back and reimagine? Joe won't give a fixed number. If things run well, leave them alone. If you're making no money or working eighty hours when you wanted forty, step back sooner. Working on the business pulls you away from working in it, so pick a rhythm that fits your revenue and your energy.

Log the problems, then fix by impact

Here's the habit Joe pushes most. Keep a log. When something breaks, write it down in one place instead of fixing it on impulse. Come back later and sort by impact. Skip the big lifts that barely move the needle. Fix the small things that free your time or keep clients happy. If a fix takes five minutes, do it now. A typo on the site gets one click, and it's done. A code rebuild waits for real time on the calendar.

The other half of this is letting go of perfect. Joe finds ten problems and wants to fix eleven. He holds back on purpose. Clients notice your flaws far less than you do. Chasing a flawless site or offer stalls the launch and traps you in a loop. Ship the imperfect version, then improve it with what you learn. This mindset connects to how to time block to protect your focus, since your calendar is where these small fixes either happen or pile up.

A business built on overwork is not sustainable

Joe has watched startups run on overwork, where the model only works if everyone grinds sixty hours a week. He calls that a plan to fail. When a big chance or a hard week arrives, there's no bandwidth left, so people quit. Seasons of heavy lifting are fine. Joe is writing a book while launching a business, and some weeks are brutal. A business that only runs on burnout is broken. If your model needs eighty-hour weeks forever, you don't have a model. You're borrowing from the future.

This lines up with what I hear across the show. When Joe surveyed more than three hundred solopreneurs, three struggles rose to the top: lead gen, sales, and time management. Solving them starts with knowing who you serve, which is why defining your ideal client makes the rest easier. And it all begins where I began, building something on my own terms instead of someone else's.

Key takeaways

  • Start with step zero. Set your life goals before you design the business that pays for them.
  • Run the Solopreneur Success Cycle as a flywheel: envision, plan, set up, execute, learn, refine, decide, adjust.
  • Execute and learn at the same time. Your first plan will break when it meets the real world.
  • Keep a log of problems. Fix by impact, not by order. Five-minute fixes get done now.
  • Let go of perfect. Clients notice your flaws far less than you do.
  • If your model needs eighty-hour weeks forever, it is not a model. Build in bandwidth.
About the guest

Joe Rando

Joe Rando is the founder and CEO of LifeStarr, a hub built for the millions of people running one-person businesses. He is co-author of Solopreneur Business for Dummies and co-hosts The Aspiring Solopreneur podcast with Carly Ries. On this episode he shared his Solopreneur Success Cycle, a step-by-step loop for building a solo business that serves your life instead of running you into the ground.
