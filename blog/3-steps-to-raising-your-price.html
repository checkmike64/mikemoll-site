How to raise your prices without adding more work

In this masterclass, I walk through the three steps I use with coaching clients to raise their price without adding deliverables: depth, desire and direction. Then we cut the work that serves nobody, and I share how one email marketer went from $1,300 to $6,000 a month.

Short answer

How do you raise your prices without adding more work?

Make your offer worth more to the buyer, then cut the work that doesn't matter. Learn what happens to your customer if their problem stays unsolved, use those fears in your marketing and sales calls, and pitch a simple three to five step process that leads with what the client gets. Then list every task you deliver and drop the ones that help neither you nor the client, often about 30% of them, while the price stays the same.

Key takeaways
  • You can often charge more for what you already do, even with three quarters of what you deliver now.

Do you have to add more work to raise your price?

Usually you don't. Most service owners believe a higher price means they have to offer more things. Sometimes that's true, because some offers are missing a piece the client needs. More often, what you do already creates enough value to charge more, even with three quarters of what you deliver now.

Here's what I hear from coaching clients. "My competitor charges this, and I don't have as much experience, so I have to charge less." Or: "If I only offer the one thing I'm great at, people won't see the value. It needs to be full service." Agency owners say this a lot. If they run Google Ads, they think they also need SEO, Facebook ads, websites and funnels.

I ran my marketing agency for 11 years. People asked all the time if we did Facebook, social media content or graphic design training. We said no to every one of them. We did Google Ads, and if you wanted someone excellent at Google Ads, I was the person to talk to. I built the whole company on that.

Why does charging too little burn you out?

A low price gets everyone to say yes, and then the work wears you down. I see founders, small teams and big teams burn out all the time. They want every buyer to say yes, so they drop the price and promise the most they can for the least money.

Then delivery starts, and it's far more than they bargained for. They have to bring in outside experts for technical work they aren't good at. The more time, software and contractors the work takes, the more it eats into your margin, and your motivation to work goes down with it.

That's why it pays to be confident in your offer. You want to earn as much from it as you can while still giving the client the change you promised. You also get a calmer business. Once you believe that what you do creates a lot of value for someone, it gets much easier to raise your price with confidence.

How do you understand a customer well enough to charge more?

Step one is depth. Learn your customer's fears, worries and motives, and what makes them say yes. If you skip this, everything you say comes from what you want them to understand, and in sales that doesn't work. If a buyer doesn't feel you understand their problem, they won't trust that you have a path forward for them.

The simplest exercise I give coaching clients is this one. Imagine what happens to this person if the problem they bring you isn't solved. Look at one month, three months, six months and 12 months.

At one month it nags at them. They're still sitting through sales calls with companies that do what you do. They're reading blogs and watching YouTube videos, and maybe trying to fix it themselves outside their zone of genius. They feel irritated and a little down. By around six months, the cost is high. In some cases, by 12 months the business is gone. Walk through it and you'll find the fears and headaches that make your work worth more.

How do you make a buyer want you to solve their problem?

Step two is desire. Show the person you understand them, so they want the problem solved and want you to be the one who solves it. Take the points that stood out most in the depth exercise and put them into your home page, sales page, landing pages, marketing material and discovery calls.

It doesn't always have to be a statement. Say you think, "I bet all my customers face the same fear." Ask about that fear as a question on the discovery call.

When your questions fit their exact situation, and you share a case study of someone who went from where they are to where they want to be, they see themselves in the story and start to trust you. That's the desire you're after. They want the problem gone, and they want you to be the person who makes it go away.

How should you explain your process when you pitch?

Step three is direction. Package your work into a three, four or five step process, and lead each step with what the client gets out of it. A lot of people pitch with every bell and whistle: we'll do the research, put it into this, write the ads, start the ad account and optimize. Most customers don't care about the small steps. They care that you have a proven process.

So skip the list of 30 line items. Each step should show how the client gets from the one before it to the next, with an obvious door into the next step.

A process you designed, one that's helped people like them get from A to B, takes away some of the risk. They see a path and a plan, and they think, "We just need to get into their flow." Depth, desire and direction together give the buyer belief that you're the person to do it.

Which deliverables can you cut and keep the same price?

Cut anything that doesn't serve you or the client. List every task you do for a customer and ask who it serves and how. If it makes the work easier for you, like an onboarding sequence or a set way of doing research, keep it. If it makes the experience or the result stronger for the buyer, keep it too.

More often than not, about 30% of the tasks serve nobody. They're in there because you saw them somewhere else, maybe at a company or agency you used to work for, and you brought the process over.

If a task doesn't make delivery easier, doesn't move the client toward the result, and doesn't make the client like you more and stay longer, kill it. Then leave the price where it is. Fewer people, less time and less energy go in, the client still gets the result, and you make the same or more.

What happens when you cut deliverables and raise the price?

One of my clients runs an email marketing business. He used to pitch by listing everything he'd do, hoping it would get buyers excited enough to trust him. He sold list management, automations, sequences and three emails a week in the brand's voice for $1,300 a month. Lots of people said yes.

When I first met him, he was ready to quit. He was burnt out, wanted time with his wife and three kids, and had no energy left. He'd boxed himself into ten different jobs.

We worked out what his clients needed to get a great result and cut everything else. Then we looked at how much revenue his work created for clients. What he produced was worth about ten times what he charged. Once he saw that, it was easy for him to say he wasn't charging enough. We rebuilt the offer with fewer items and raised it to a $6,000 a month retainer. We also gave his steps a name and a brand. Now buyers see the vision, and he closes a very high percentage of his sales.

What can you do this week?

  1. Write down what happens to your customer if their problem isn't solved in one, three, six and 12 months.
  2. Take the fears that stand out and add them to your home page, sales page and discovery call questions.
  3. Pick one case study of a client who went from where your buyer is to where they want to be.
  4. Turn your task list into a three to five step process, and start each step with what the client gets.
  5. List every task you deliver, ask who it serves, and cut the ones that serve nobody while the price stays the same.
