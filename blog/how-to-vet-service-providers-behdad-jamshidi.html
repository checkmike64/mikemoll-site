How to vet a marketing agency before you hire one

Behdad Jamshidi is a marketing broker who has vetted hundreds of agencies and matches them with businesses. He told me what the good ones do on the first call, in the proposal and at onboarding, and what agency owners can copy to win clients and keep them.

Key takeaways
  • On the first call, a good agency gives a short intro and spends the rest of the time on the client's goals, competitors and past agencies.
  • Most agencies are good at two or three things, and the ones that say so keep clients longer.
  • A strong marketer who learns your business can beat a niche agency whose marketing is weak.

What does a marketing broker see when vetting agencies?

Behdad Jamshidi works as a marketing broker at CJAM Marketing. He matches businesses with agencies, and over the last three years he has vetted hundreds of them. When we recorded this, about 50 agencies were on his partner list.

He got into it because he ran out of time. He started out selling websites, Google Ads and SEO, then went looking for agencies to hand the work to. That's when he saw the pattern. Most agencies didn't understand business, and most business owners didn't understand marketing. The two sides would sit in the same meeting and still not agree on what to do next.

His day job helps. He's a customer solution architect, an engineer who talks with leaders at companies of 100 to 1,000 people and builds them a technology roadmap. Because he sits in the middle, he hears feedback that the agency and the business wouldn't get on their own.

I've seen the same split for nine years. A client asks to raise their conversion rate and then asks what a click-through rate is. Behdad's advice for agency owners is to remember there are five or six ways to do most marketing, and to help the client pick one based on risk, cost and who they are.

What should happen on the first call with an agency?

The agency should spend most of the call asking about your business. Behdad says the biggest mistake he sees, from junior agencies and some expert ones too, is an agency that talks about itself. It lists what it does, its logos and its clients, and 15 or 20 minutes in, the client still hasn't said a word.

Behdad flips that. He gives the same two-minute intro he gave me, and the rest of the meeting is about the client: their goals, their competitors, what they've tried, the agencies they've used, and what they liked and didn't like. In the last five minutes he says which agencies he thinks can help. You can't position your agency well until you know what matters to the customer.

As a coach who listens to sales calls, I see the opposite when someone wants the client badly. They steer the call toward the yes. That brings in money now, but if the client isn't set up to succeed, it hurts the trust and how long the contract lasts.

Should an agency admit what it isn't good at?

Yes. Behdad says being open about it helps you keep clients longer. Most agencies are good at two or three things. He sees it most with full-service agencies, which tend to show up around 15 people.

So double down on your strengths and tell clients you're kind of average at the rest. If a client needs email or affiliate work and that isn't your strength, find a partner to send it to. Taking it on anyway means hiring for it or pulling someone off work they're good at, and then nobody does well. An SEO agency should even tell a client when the rest of their funnel isn't ready. Behdad says those clients come back when they're ready.

I learned this the hard way. In my second, third and fourth years of business, we'd get halfway through a project and I'd wonder why we were building an automation sequence at all. We took it on for fear the client would leave for someone who did it all. Behdad says only two setups cover it all well: a very large agency with a client who has a very large budget, or a client with an internal CMO who brings a group of niche agencies together.

Is a niche agency always better?

No. Behdad tells business owners that a strong marketer can beat a niche agency whose marketing is weak. A niche agency knows its industry well, but it can get so specialized that it stops looking at the big picture and misses ideas from other industries.

I asked him about this because I spent years running ads for anybody before I went hyper niche. His advice for a sales call without a niche is to go back to his first point and understand the business. When people feel understood, that's a big plus in your direction. Then explain that working across industries lets you see things a narrow agency may miss. A strong marketer can tell a client that within two months, they'll have worked out the customer profiles and who to target.

When a business owner asks Behdad for a niche agency, he tells them he'd pick the strong marketer and give them three or four months. In the long run, they'll be ahead of an agency that only went niche because it couldn't get business any other way.

The same idea, from the founder's sideKnowing who you serve still matters when you sell. This episode with Susie Ippolito covers how to define your ideal client.

How should an agency structure a proposal?

Open with the client's goals and ask them to confirm you got them right. Behdad says the first five minutes and the last five minutes of a presentation are where attention sticks, so don't spend the opening talking about yourself.

After your intro slide, show the business goals and objectives the client gave you on the discovery call. Then ask, "Can you confirm that we got this right?" If they say no, you know you missed something, and they can clarify it on the spot. Build the rest on those goals: here's the plan to reach them, here's why we're the right partner, and here's what it costs. Layered that way, the client thinks: they understand me, they have a plan, and they can guide me from A to B.

Then present it. Behdad sees agencies email the proposal and wait, expecting a client who knows less about marketing to read it and agree. He's noticed that agencies who don't present have much lower close rates. Present it first and send it after, unless the client wants it beforehand. You can't handle an objection if you aren't there when it comes up.

What makes a client pick one agency over another?

They pick the agency they trust to understand them and get them to their goal. For the "why us" part of a pitch, Behdad points to your values and where they line up with the client's, any awards, and case studies from their niche or something close. Walk them through what you did, how you thought about it and how you communicated, so they know what working with you will be like.

Business is a human-to-human interaction, he says, so look up the client's volunteer work and charities and find where you line up.

I see proposals with two or three pages of deliverables and 15 or 20 bullet points. Clients don't care about those. They want to know what the process and the experience will look like, and whether you seem credible enough to get them to the finish line. Behdad added that every extra bullet point invites the client to ask if they can take it out. If they ask for detail, give it. If they don't, start at the high level.

What does good agency onboarding look like?

Good onboarding is the same process for every client. Behdad asks about onboarding when he interviews agencies, and he says the best ones have processes in place. They send the same documents to every customer, ask them to fill them out, and answer the questions clients tend to have.

The first few days are where it gets messy. The client says yes and they're excited, and then they have to hand over access to things they can't find. Someone set up a Google Analytics account ten years ago and nobody can get into it anymore.

Behdad's advice is to start with a base outline, even a Word or Google document, and add to it each time you find something missing. Over time you get a process that works for 90 to 95% of your clients. It makes it easy to get the information you need and start the work, and you can automate more of it as it settles. Every client then gets the same result at the end.

Does an agency need to keep growing?

Not always. Behdad says some of his best agencies have 20 people, take on about 20 clients a year and don't want to get bigger. They know exactly who they are, and they vet clients to make sure they get the right ones. Behdad says you can build a very profitable business that way.

Growth comes with new costs. Around 15 or 20 people, Behdad sees a big shift: the owner has to step out and get the business to run by itself. Another shift comes at 40 or 50 people, when you need new processes and systems. Each stage brings new expenses, new hires and new processes to build. So decide where you want to go before you chase the next stage. Behdad's view is that taking care of the client matters more than getting bigger.

I agree with him. Online, almost everything aimed at agency owners is about eight figures and hypergrowth. Every new plateau brings a learning curve and a new set of problems, and at 40 or 50 people you almost have to rebuild the business. I'm motivated the other way. I want to start small and be massively profitable.

What can you do this week?

  1. On your next sales call, keep your intro to two minutes and spend the rest asking about the client's goals, competitors and past agencies.
  2. Write down the two or three things your agency does best, and find a partner for the work you're only average at.
  3. Start your next proposal with a slide of the client's goals, and ask them to confirm you got it right.
  4. Book a time to present that proposal live before you email it.
  5. Cut the list of deliverables down to what the process and the experience will look like, and give the detail when the client asks.
  6. Put your onboarding steps in one document you send to every new client, and add to it each time something is missing.

If you're thinking about what size business you want to build, my episode with Joe Rando on the solopreneur success cycle is a good next listen.

They're not just going off the cuff based on each individual customer. They're going, "Here's the documents. Please fill this out." It's systematized, it's the same for every single customer, and it answers all the customer's questions.

Once you start with a base outline, you can iterate on that: oh, we missed this, or we missed that. Over time you have a process that works for pretty much 90, 95% of your customers, and it makes it really easy for you to get information and get started, versus trying to figure out, oh, we need this, we need that. You have to have those systems in place. It could be a Word document, a Google document, whatever it is. Just have a process in place so that you can iterate and automate that part of the process as much as you can, and you get the same result at the end.

18:04 Growth, or the right size

Mike: Yeah, absolutely. On behalf of agency owners, what did I not ask you that they should think about, know, prepare or build? Anything that I missed where you're like, it would be really helpful if agencies knew this about selling their services to businesses?

Behdad: I've noticed this thing as agencies grow. When you're one, two, three people, that's totally fine. You get to about 15 or 20, and there's this fundamental shift where, as an owner, you need to start stepping out of your business and get the business to run by itself. The people that do that successfully are the ones that are able to get out of the business at that point. I see another one happen at the 40 or 50 person level too. You get to a certain scale where you need to have certain processes and systems in place.

So just be aware. Everyone wants to grow. Growth is important, but with growth comes a whole new set of expenses, people you need to hire and processes you need to build. Always think about where you want to be and where you want to go, and have those systems in place. But sometimes it's okay to just be a certain size. Some of my best agencies are like, "We're 20 people. We don't want to get bigger. We can cater to 20 customers a year, and that's all we want to do. We're just vetting to make sure we get the right customers."

Those agencies know what they want, and they've figured out exactly who they are. So I think it's really important to think about yourself as an agency owner and where you want to go. It's not always growth that matters. It's going, I'm going to focus on the customer. I'm going to take care of the customer. If I do that at 20 people, for 20 businesses, all the time, and do it well, I can make a very profitable business out of that.

Mike: Yeah, that's interesting that you say that, because when agency owners and marketers are on the internet, everything on YouTube, everything is about getting to eight figures, hypergrowth, do this, do that. I have conversations with people all the time, and my first question is, why? "Well, because it's widely accepted that once you get to a million dollars a year, you're super happy." My profit margins are outrageous. I do 650,000. I pull a ridiculous salary. All my life expenses are paid for. I have no stress, and I work 60 hours a month.

It's really interesting that you say the best-performing ones look like that, because they can focus on how we make our process better, how we maximize profit while still delivering a good product. Whereas the people in hypergrowth at 15 people, there's this evolution, all these extra things you have to add. Then you get to 40, 50 and you have to almost recreate the entire business. And every time you reach that next plateau, there's the learning curve, the drop-off, the problems, the challenges.

So yeah, it's interesting that you say that, because I'm very motivated in the opposite way to growth. I'm like, how do I start small and be massively profitable? That's what I care about.
