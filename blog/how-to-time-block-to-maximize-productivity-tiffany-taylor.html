How to time block your day around your goals

Tiffany Taylor coaches business owners to run their week from a calendar. We covered how to set goals that pass the SMART test, put your life on the calendar first, size each block to your attention span and group work into theme days.

How do you time block your day?

Start with a vision you can see and a few goals that pass the SMART test, then break each goal into tasks and put them on a calendar. Put sleep, rest, exercise and family time on first, and fit work around them. Start with 45-minute blocks and adjust to how long you can hold focus. If you skip a block, drag it to another day and pull a different task into today.

Why doesn't a to-do list work for business owners?

Tiffany Taylor says a to-do list leaves out the work that grows a business. Every time she takes one apart, sales and prospecting are missing. She's known in her space for being against to-do lists, and she calls them the most inefficient way to look at your time when you're trying to reach $50K a month. So she moves her clients onto a digital calendar.

I asked her why the hard work drops off the list. Her answer was about how you define productivity. If productivity means getting more things done, you'll make a list of everything you could do and work through it one item at a time. If it means moving the needle with the least effort, you'll plan your week in a very different way.

She ties this to how you run the business, too. One owner asks, "What is the most I can do to get my outcome?" and tries to do all the things. Another asks, "What is the least I can do to reach my goals?" Those two questions lead to two very different weeks.

What should you do before you block your calendar?

Write down a vision you can see, set goals from it, and only then break the goals into tasks. Tiffany says most owners skip the first two steps and go straight to the list. Problems start when the list, the goals and the vision don't line up.

Tiffany says sometimes upwards of 80% of a to-do list isn't helping you move toward your goals. You knock off every item, the needle stays put, and your confidence drops.

For the business, she likes a big two: revenue and number of customers. Every smaller goal or task should tie straight back to those. Then each goal goes through the SMART test. It should be specific, measurable, possible to achieve, realistic and relevant to your vision, with a timeline. If it fails a check, don't call it a goal yet. A good goal should stress you out a little. If it doesn't, Tiffany says you didn't make it big enough.

Why do owners set goals and then avoid them?

Tiffany puts it down to a vision that isn't clear yet. Without one, people set goals, feel resistance and can't tell why, so they avoid the goals. Do that enough times and you build a habit of setting goals and missing them.

She sees goal setting as a daily trust exercise with yourself. Self-trust works like a relationship with a partner or a spouse. You make trust deposits each time you do the things you said you'd do.

This matches what I see in people one step before they'd get to Tiffany. Very few of them have goals at all, and without goals it's almost impossible to talk about how they spend their time. I used to love the buzz of busy work. I'd tell myself, "Look, I did these 19 things today," while nothing moved forward.

Putting goal tasks on a calendar is where this gets tested. Tiffany calls it a confronting moment, because now you're committing time and energy. If you feel resistance, you have to face it. On a to-do list, you can just forget about it.

How do you hand off work you're not good at?

Hire people who are better than you at it, and give them trust a little at a time. Tiffany's last step after setting goals is to ask two questions. What is the fewest tasks I can do to reach them? Who can I add to my team to help?

That second question matters when you're the be-all and end-all of the business. At some points I've had seven or eight contractors working on different things. My clearest example is copywriting. I know my story and I can explain it when I talk, but I can't write it well enough for a website. Handing that job off gave me a stronger site.

Tiffany says if you're serious about growing, you have to hire people who are better than you. That means taking a slice of humble pie first.

Most owners are willing, she said. Trust is what slows them down. Give a little, enough for the person to keep earning more. Tell them that's what you're doing and what evidence you need to see before you hand over more.

What goes on your calendar first?

Your life goes on first: sleep, rest, exercise, date nights and the kids' activities. Work fills in after that. Tiffany says sleep is the last thing she sees people add, and it needs to go first.

Start by being honest about your office hours. If you don't set those boundaries for yourself, no one else will set them for you. "I'm never not working" is a line Tiffany hears a lot, and she doesn't want it to be yours.

Then plan what you need in your off hours to rest before you go back to work. Tiffany says rest is as important as work, because it affects the quality of your work. In her book, rest means reading, meditating, exercising and getting enough sleep. TV, movies and nights out drinking go in a different bucket. Rest on the calendar gives you steady energy through the day and focus periods you can count on.

She color codes the calendar so you can see those boundaries at a glance.

How do you start time blocking if you've never used a calendar?

Let the calendar follow you for a week before you plan anything. Track what you do, hour by hour, as it happens. Tiffany says this makes you face the reality of time. You're always doing something, and you can't store an hour today and use it tomorrow.

Expect to be frustrated by what you see. She says you need to sit in that pain, because that's where change starts. Every moment should be on the calendar, since you're doing something with every moment of the day.

Color makes the week easy to read. On Tiffany's calendar, blue is work, lavender is relaxing and self-care, green is exercise, and another color marks her weekly personal development time. One look tells her if she worked too much or if her meetings were spread over 12 hours.

Then she adjusts. If a week felt horrible because she worked too long on one day, she shortens the hours the next week. If she needed a nap by Tuesday afternoon, she schedules one.

What happens when you skip a time block?

Drag it to another day, and pull a different task into today. I told Tiffany my fear with this system. On day two I miss something, I don't have the energy, and I decide the whole thing has failed. A lot of people are all or nothing like that, and I'm one of them.

Her fix uses the drag and drop in a tool like Google Calendar. Say it's Tuesday afternoon and you don't feel like sending emails for an hour. Move that block to tomorrow. Then take something you planned for another day and drag it into today. You still get done something that had to get done, and your time is still accounted for. You even get a small guilty pleasure from skipping the thing.

As for how long the change takes, the tracking week is the easy part, since you're only writing down what you did. Some people track for two weeks. After that, Tiffany says, they're ready to start blocking.

How long should a time block be?

Start with 45 minutes and adjust to your attention span. Tiffany says there's no set length. Watch your own workflow and notice when your attention starts to fade. For some people that's half an hour, and for others it's four hours.

Short blocks don't work well because of something called attention residue. Tiffany says it takes about 16 to 23 minutes on average to get fully absorbed in a new task. A 20-minute block ends just as you're getting into it. A two-minute phone call in the middle of an hour of focus can cost you 16 to 23 minutes of energy too. She works with a lot of people with ADHD, and they can get into hyperfocus if they dive into something for four hours or more.

Her other tip is theme days. Give one day to CEO work, one to marketing and one to content, and keep calls to one or two days. The fewer times you switch tasks or roles, the more you get done. That helps me, since I run a marketing agency and a coaching business. It's Paul Graham's idea of makers and managers. As a maker, I need a whole day with nothing else on it for creative work.

What can you do this week?

  1. Write down your vision where you'll see it, then pick your big two goals, such as revenue and number of customers.
  2. Run each goal through the SMART test and hold back any that fail a check.
  3. Let your calendar follow you for one week and color code what you did.
  4. Put sleep, rest, exercise and family time on next week's calendar before any work.
  5. Schedule your goal tasks in 45-minute blocks, then adjust to how long you can hold focus.
  6. When you skip a block, drag it to another day and pull a different task into today.

Tiffany's last point was about guilt, the top complaint she hears about time. She says it doesn't have to be that way, and sometimes changing the tool you use changes how you talk to yourself. Mike: Got it. Rest and relaxation is kind of a personal thing. It depends on what activities you partake in. But what have you seen have the most success in terms of time boxing work activities? Is it like, "Hey, I block in these 20-minute segments to do different things"? Is it longer boxes? Obviously it depends on the nature of what you're doing, but just as a generalist?

Tiffany: Here's the thing with time blocking. There's no secret formula for how long a time block should be. The key is to monitor your own workflow and notice when your attention starts to taper off. For some people, it's half an hour. For some people, it's 4 hours. There's this thing called attention residue within productivity. As soon as you start a new task, it takes you about 16 to 23 minutes on average to be fully absorbed in that task. If you do a time block of just 20 minutes, you're barely into that task by the time you go to switch. So you're not really putting your full energy into it.

This also goes for a distraction. If you have an hour focus period and you get distracted, you take a phone call in that time, it's going to take you maybe 2 minutes of distracted time, but it's going to take you 16 to 23 minutes of energy that you would have been putting into that original task. I recommend you start with a 45-minute time block and see how you go. Maybe you feel like working more. That's excellent. If it's less, that's good, too. But match the time blocks to your actual attention span. That's what's most important.

Now, someone with ADHD, for example. I work with a lot of people with ADHD, and I know quite a few entrepreneurs have this as well. ADHD means you have the ability to get into hyperfocus, but it takes 4 hours. You can have completely undistracted time if you dive into something for 4 hours or more. But if you do the smaller tasks, it becomes more difficult.

19:00 Theme days, makers and managers

Tiffany: A few other tips I've seen that have been really helpful: having theme days. So one day you focus on CEO, another day is just marketing day. Maybe another day is just content day. Maybe just one or two days for calls, rather than having them scattered throughout the week. The least amount of times you can switch tasks or roles throughout the day is going to lead to maximum productivity.

Mike: That's a great tip. That's super helpful for me, because I've got the marketing agency and the coaching thing. So only take coaching calls on certain days, and then product building time on certain days. That's the Paul Graham concept of makers versus managers, and being aware of what type of brain you are when it comes to work. For me, as a maker, I can only accomplish a handful of things in a day. If I'm in a bunch of meetings, my brain never gets situated back to be able to do product development or sales and marketing development. I just completely tune out.

So yeah, if it's a creative thing, for me personally, I need the entire day with nothing else on it. If I go to start something creative and I know I have a call in a few hours, the whole time I'm going to be thinking about that call and be afraid I'm going to miss it. Because if I'm at the computer, even if I have a meeting come up, I need an alarm on my phone to remind me, because I will just be so absorbed in my work.
