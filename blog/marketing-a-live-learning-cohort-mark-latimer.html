How to market an online course as a solopreneur

Mark Latimer set out to reach 3,000 solopreneurs with his ChatGPT courses in a year, without a big team or a big ad budget. On this live coaching episode we worked out how: sell through partners, give copies away on purpose, ask for reviews and referrals, and answer questions on video.

Short answer

How do you market an online course when you're a team of one?

Sell through people who already have your audience. Find businesses that sell something next to your course and split the revenue with them, give free copies to people who run big communities, and ask every happy student for a review and a referral. Work out what one subscriber is worth to you first, so you know how much you can give away to get the next one.

Key takeaways
  • Make the course good first. Mark interviews beta users when they finish and uses what they say to improve it.
  • Sell through partners whose audience would love your course and who don't sell the same thing, for a revenue share.
  • A recorded course costs nothing to copy, so give it away on purpose to people who run communities.
  • Happy students rarely tell anyone. Ask for a review in an automated email when they finish.
  • Know what one subscriber is worth to you, then reward referrals with something that costs you less.
  • Don't overwhelm buyers with a thousand prompts or 85 hours of video. Sell an outcome a student had from the course.

Why does the course have to be good before you market it?

Mark runs Art of ChatGPT, where he teaches solopreneurs to use ChatGPT in their business. His free course, iPhone Mastery for ChatGPT, starts on your phone, so using it becomes a daily habit. When we recorded this, his goal was to serve 3,000 solopreneurs in a year.

When I asked how he planned to get there, he started with the product. The course has to be good enough that people finish it and tell someone. He's interviewing beta users when they finish, and he uses what they say to improve the course and to write content that turns visitors into active users.

I agree with that order. Every idea below works better when the people who take the course come out the other side with a win.

How do you sell a course through someone else's audience?

Find people who sell something next to your course and let them sell it for you. I'm doing this with my course on how to get booked on podcasts. One company I talk to helps people become authors. They help write, publish and market the book, and one of their services is booking authors on podcasts. Some of their clients will never pay for that service, so I asked them to sell my course to those clients at whatever price they like and keep a percentage. A few PR firms are doing the same.

The test is simple: their audience would love what you do, and you aren't competing for the same dollars.

For Mark, that means marketing agencies and training companies with a lot of small-business clients. They can sell his ChatGPT courses as part of their own programs on something like a 50/50 revenue share. Once it's set up, it's a channel he doesn't have to work.

The same idea, for servicesGetting booked as a podcast guest works the same way. The host already has the audience, and they hear you on a show they trust. The podcast guesting workshop covers how to do it.

Why give a digital course away for free?

Once a course is recorded, another copy costs you nothing. That changes what you can afford to give away.

My podcast course sells for $375, and it paid for itself in the first four sales. So when someone I want on board asks what it costs, I send them a copy. They get a $375 course, and it costs me nothing to send.

Mark can do the same with bigger groups. Donate ten copies as prizes at a conference or an association event. Give five copies to someone who runs a large community, for their best members, and ask for feedback. If they like it, set up an affiliate deal. You earn trust with that community before you ask it for anything.

Why won't a good course sell itself?

Mark said a course that's done well should market itself, and a good course does a lot of the work. The catch is that happy customers stay quiet. When someone loves a course, they keep it to themselves and mention it only if a friend asks. When someone hates a course, they post about it.

So build the ask into the course. Set up an automated email that goes out when someone reaches the end. Ask for a review or a testimonial, and tell them why it matters: it helps you reach more people like them. Asking in plain, grateful words has gotten me good responses.

The course has to respect people's time too. I once paid for a personal branding course from a big name that was a string of YouTube videos with no direction. Mark has spent a lot of time editing his so people want to keep going, and that's the kind of course people finish and recommend.

How do referral rewards work for a course?

Give people a reason to share, and make sharing one click. Here's what I do with my newsletter. Every subscriber gets a link to share. If 20 people sign up through it, they get a free half hour with me to solve a big problem in their business. I've charged up to $1,000 for one hour.

Mark can do the same with what he already has. Get 20 people into the course and you get the other courses free, or a spot in a program. If the first course helped someone, they'll share it when sharing is easy.

I can give that away because I know what a subscriber is worth to me. When we recorded this, every 25 people on my list brought in about $275 on average, through courses or one-off consulting. Once you know that figure, you can compare a referral reward with what ads would cost you. Referrals come with proof built in, too. A friend posting "I took this and it was great" converts better than any post you write about yourself.

What should a course ad say?

Mark asked how I'd advertise his course on Instagram or Facebook. I'd lead with one outcome a student got. Most AI course ads offer a thousand prompts for $27, or a course with 85 hours of material. As a solopreneur, I don't want more to get through. I want the answer.

Take three stories from your exit interviews: the hours a student saved each week, or the agency they stopped paying because they could do the job themselves. People see themselves in other people's outcomes and think, "I want that." The ad is the teaser that gets that reaction. The landing page gives the details of what they get.

How do you get seen without paying for ads?

Answer people's questions on video where they're already asking them. Go through Reddit threads and Facebook groups and find people who are stuck. Record a short Loom of you solving their problem in ChatGPT, step by step. Loom lets you add a call to action to the video, so it can point to your course. If you spend seven minutes solving someone's problem, it's fair to mention your course at the end.

I do a version of this every week. I used to write long answers to people's questions. Now I answer 8 to 10 a week on Loom. Each one is YouTube content, it gives me clips for other channels, and I run the transcript through a GPT I built for my brand voice to draft a blog post.

Mark added a recording tip from his side: record the full screen every time. You can crop later, but you can't go back and get the part you didn't record.

How long before posting consistently pays off?

For me it took about 60 days. I posted on LinkedIn three to four times a week and shared Instagram stories fairly regularly. Then I landed two contracts, possibly a third, worth about $45K, in 10 days. One of those leads told me they'd been keeping an eye on my content and had seen one of my ads.

Repetition is what makes people remember what you do. People start to think "Mike's the guy who does that thing." For Mark, the goal is that people think "Mark's the guy who makes AI easy."

At our stage, with no full team and nobody else as the face of the brand, that means making a lot of content for a while. Mark is planning for it with production blocks in his calendar, an assistant who finds questions on Reddit for him to answer, and editing he'll hand off once his course on editing with ChatGPT is recorded.

What can you do this week?

  1. Write down three businesses that sell something next to your course, and ask each one to sell it for a revenue share.
  2. Pick five people who run communities your buyers are in, and send each of them a few free copies for their best members.
  3. Add an email that goes out when someone finishes the course and asks for a review in plain, grateful words.
  4. Work out what one subscriber is worth to you, then set a referral reward that costs you less than that.
  5. Answer one question a day on video in a group where your buyers spend time, and point to the course at the end.
