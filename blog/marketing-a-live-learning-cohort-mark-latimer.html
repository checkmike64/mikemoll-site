How to Market a Live Learning Cohort

January 15, 202533 minLive Coaching
Listen on Spotify Apple Podcasts Watch on YouTube

Mark Latimer runs Art of ChatGPT. He teaches solopreneurs how to use ChatGPT in their businesses. His goal this year is to serve 3,000 people across his free and paid courses. He came to this live coaching session with one problem to solve. He needed a way to fill a cohort that big without burning himself out on paid ads.

Here is the short version of the plan we built on the call. Borrow other people's audiences. Give your digital course away on purpose. Turn your helpful videos into the marketing itself. Each of those moves costs little and compounds over time. The rest of this post walks through how Mark can put them to work.

Start with a course worth talking about

Mark's first instinct was right. The course has to be strong before any marketing matters. He runs exit interviews with his beta users and fixes the weak spots they point to. A good course carries some of its own weight, up to a point. Here is the catch. People complain louder than they praise. A bad course gets more word of mouth than a great one, because a great one gets tucked in a back pocket and forgotten. So you build a course people love, then you give that love a push out the door.

Borrow audiences instead of building from zero

The fastest path to 3,000 people runs through tribes that already exist. Find someone whose audience overlaps with yours but whose offer does not compete with yours.

I did this with my own course on how to get booked on podcasts. I sold it to an agency that helps people publish books. Their clients want podcast bookings. The agency does not teach that skill, so there is no overlap. They sell my finished course, take a percentage, and I reach their entire list without lifting a finger. I earned my production cost back in four sales, so every deal after that is upside.

Mark can run the same play. Done-for-you marketing agencies and PR firms sit on rooms full of clients who would use his AI training. A 50/50 revenue share turns each of those partners into a channel. He records one podcast episode with a partner, shows the product, and lets them sell it to their people. Those clients already match his target: solopreneurs with an iPhone who want to master ChatGPT. That is why it helps to get clear on exactly who the course is for before you pick partners.

Use the zero-cost nature of a digital product

A digital course costs nothing to copy. Once you have earned back the production, every extra sale or gift is free. That changes how you can market it.

Gifting fits Mark's brand well, since he teaches gratitude. So give five copies to a tribe owner for their top people. Collect their feedback. Donate copies as prizes for conferences or industry associations. Each gift builds trust and proof at no cost to you. When the feedback comes back strong, you turn those fans into affiliates. This is close to how one guest turned free content into paid offers once he knew people valued the work.

Build a social-proof engine

One happy buyer tends to stay quiet. You have to ask for the review.

Set up a message that fires after purchase. Frame it with gratitude. Something like, "We want to get this into more hands. A short review would mean a lot." That framing pulls strong responses because it ties the ask to helping others.

Then add a referral reward that costs you nothing. Tell your buyers this. Share your link, get 20 people to sign up, and you unlock more of my products for free. Your products cost nothing to give, so the reward is free to you and worth real money to them. Better yet, their share is social proof. A buyer telling their friends "I took this and it was great" converts far better than any ad you write about yourself.

Know what a subscriber is worth

Once you track the number, marketing choices get simple. On my list, every 20 to 25 subscribers is worth about $275 on average. Some become a course sale. Some become a consulting call.

When you know that figure, you can pick your lever with confidence. You can run ads, or you can give away a product that costs you nothing and reach the same result. That math is also how I decide when to spend on paid traffic, the same way I coached a guest through marketing an offer on Facebook.

Sell the outcome, not the pile of features

Solopreneurs do not want 1,000 prompts or 85 hours of video. That volume feels like one more thing they will never finish. They want the result, and they want it fast.

So in your ads and posts, drop the feature count. Tell a short story pulled from your exit interviews. "This course cut 15 hours a week of content work." "This person stopped hiring three vendors to do one task." People see themselves in the outcome and want it for their own business. The ad teases the result. The landing page fills in the details.

Make video your marketing engine

Mark is strong on camera, so we put that to work. Go into Reddit threads and Facebook groups where solopreneurs are stuck. Record a short Loom that solves the problem step by step inside ChatGPT. At the end, add a call to action that points to the course. A seven-minute video that solves a real problem earns the right to that CTA.

Then repurpose the recording. It becomes a YouTube video and a handful of short clips. Run the transcript through a brand-voice GPT and get a blog post out of it. One recording feeds many channels, which keeps the content flywheel turning while the team is still small.

Consistency does the compounding

None of this pays off from a single push. It pays off when you show up on a schedule. I posted on LinkedIn three to four times a week for 60 days. In one 10-day stretch, that consistency helped close around $45k in contracts. The leads all said a version of the same thing. "I have been watching your content." Repeat exposure makes you the first name people think of. Over time, Mark becomes the person who makes AI easy, and that reputation fills the cohort on its own.

Key takeaways

  • Fill a cohort by borrowing audiences. Set up 50/50 revenue-share deals with partners whose offers complement yours instead of competing with them.
  • A digital course costs nothing to copy. Gift it on purpose to build trust and proof, then turn happy users into affiliates.
  • Ask for reviews with a gratitude frame, and reward referrals with your own products, which cost you nothing to give away.
  • Track what a subscriber is worth. Once you know the number, you can choose paid ads or free gifting with confidence.
  • Sell the outcome with real stories from exit interviews. Solopreneurs want the result fast, not more hours of material.
  • Turn help into marketing. Record Loom videos that solve real problems, add a CTA, then repurpose them into YouTube, clips, and blog posts.
