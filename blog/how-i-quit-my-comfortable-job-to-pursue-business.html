How I knew it was time to quit my corporate job

I climbed fast in corporate insurance and had the things I was told to want, and I was miserable. This is the morning a panic attack in the office parking lot ended that job, what my family told me to do, and why I quit on the spot anyway.

Short answer

How do you know when it's time to quit your corporate job?

For me, the sign was a life that looked great on paper and felt miserable to live. I had climbed fast in insurance and had a company car, a home office and four weeks of vacation, and I was running on empty. One morning I had a panic attack in the office parking lot, and I quit that day with no other job lined up. My family told me to wait, and I knew that advice was the wrong answer for me. I felt at peace the moment I left.

What are the signs you've outgrown a corporate job?

For me, the first sign was that I couldn't stop asking why things were done the way they were. I saw waste everywhere, and I wanted to fix it. I started in insurance at 21, with no degree and no experience, in a pretty basic job. Rules like filling out a form so you could fill out another form made no sense to me. I asked a lot of questions the company didn't have answers for. It was a huge company, with over 3,000 employees in Canada alone.

Most of the skilled, talented people around me were there because they had a family and needed the consistency. I used that to move up. I kept asking how we could do something faster, fix it quickly, or make it simpler. Most days I had my work done before 11 a.m., and I made some enemies as the try-hard guy. Looking back, that urge to simplify is the thing that helped me most once I went out on my own.

Why do top performers get frustrated in big companies?

In my case, the reward for doing well was more work. I was a top performer in every role I had, and the company kept handing me the hardest files. After a few years in my first role, I moved up to commercial property claims, like break-ins, damage and fires.

Because I moved through files so fast, I got the cases with lots of parties involved, or ones under investigation. My target stayed the same as everyone else's. For every new claim that opened, I had to close one. That was the same rule as the person handling small petty theft claims.

So I stopped filling out my own performance review. Each year you were supposed to rate yourself, compare notes with your manager, and get a rating that set your bonus. My team thought I was crazy, and I was afraid I'd get in trouble. I told them they couldn't give me the most complicated work and hold me to the same standard as everybody else. Even then, I'd look at something that wasn't done well and ask why there couldn't be any flexibility.

What does it look like to win on paper and still be unhappy?

It looked like the life I was told to want. By my fifth year, I was in a role most people reached in their eighth or tenth. I'd negotiated a home office, a company car, a high five-figure salary and four weeks of vacation. People my age and level didn't get those things. I forced my way into them.

I lived in a posh building on Yonge Street in Toronto. On paper, I had it all. Underneath, my marriage wasn't going well, and my risk assessment job was depressing. All day I talked to people lying about how hurt they were, and to our own clients lying about how much of it was their fault.

I was checking boxes: get married, then take the next step, then the one after that. I was following the storybook version of how life is supposed to go, and I wasn't listening to myself at all. I liked the security and the status. The way I was getting them wasn't serving my soul.

What does burnout feel like before it breaks?

For me, it felt like running on empty while moving as fast as I could. I had nothing left in the tank, and I couldn't fake my way through it anymore. By then I was doing very little at work. I did the things that absolutely had to be done and left deadlines until the last moment. I drank as much caffeine as I could just to find some motivation, and I didn't want to talk to my co-workers or the clients.

I went to the office a couple of days a week just to get out. I did everything in fast-forward mode and bulldozed through each day. I figured that if I kept moving fast enough, the unhappiness couldn't catch me. I wasn't into mindfulness or meditation. Sitting still to breathe was something I rarely did, so the day it all came out started like any other day.

What happened the morning I quit?

I had a panic attack in the office parking lot, and by the end of that morning I'd quit. I drove to work, parked, and for some reason took a moment to sit and breathe. I don't know why I did it. When I did, everything I'd been holding down hit me at once, like a flood.

I thought I was having a heart attack. A co-worker of mine had died of one, so that's where my mind went. Blood rushed to my head, my ears rang, and I got dizzy. I started shaking, my breathing was off, and I couldn't focus my eyes. Then I broke down crying. It started out silent and turned hysterical.

There had been so much build-up for so long, with no way to let it out. It felt like forever, but it was probably 10 to 15 minutes. I couldn't even use my arms to open the car door.

Should you line up another job before you quit?

That's what everyone I called told me to do, and on paper they were right. When I could move again, I called my dad first, then my mom, then my wife. I told each of them I couldn't do this. I didn't like the role or the job, and I wasn't in the right life.

They each said it in their own way, and the message was the same. Stick it out, find another job, and quit once you have that security. I didn't have a lot of money put away. I wasn't great with money at the time, and I wasn't making crazy money either.

I knew they were right on paper, and I knew it was the wrong answer for me. In that moment, something in my brain clicked. I decided I would not set foot in that office to work that job for one more day.

How do you quit a job without burning the bridge?

Leave your work in good shape, even if the conversation goes badly. Mine went badly. I got out of the car with my face still red from crying, walked straight into my boss's office and asked if he had a minute. I didn't have a resignation letter. I quit on the spot, and he screamed at me.

I don't think it was about me. He was dealing with staffing issues and a new boss of his own. I said okay, went back to my desk and typed up my resignation letter. Then I made notes on where I was leaving everything and where the files were at, so they could take over my work.

Unless someone has done something horribly wrong to you, I don't think you should leave with your middle fingers up. This was me being in the wrong place, and it had nothing to do with them. My boss came to my desk later and apologized. It didn't change my mind.

What happens after you quit with no plan?

For me, the pressure showed up right away, and so did a feeling of peace. I knew I had to figure something out fast. If I was going to be this fancy entrepreneur guy, I had to start something quickly, because my runway wasn't long.

My wife was making six figures at the time, but it wasn't her plan to support our family on her own. Cutting off one of our incomes that suddenly was a shock, and people reacted poorly, including her. I was working again pretty soon after, in a sales role, and I'll save that story for another day.

Even with people disappointed or mad at me, I was so at peace. I was breathing so much easier, and leaving felt so good. It was the right choice for me. I'm sharing this for anyone who looks like they're winning on paper and feels like they're the only one losing in their soul.

What can you do this week?

  1. Write down what your job looks like on paper, then write down how it feels to live it.
  2. List the rules and targets at work that make no sense to you, and see which ones you keep fighting.
  3. Take one quiet minute in the car or at your desk before work, and notice what comes up.
  4. When people you trust give you advice, ask yourself whether it's right on paper or right for you.
  5. If you decide to leave, write down where every piece of your work stands so the next person can pick it up.
