How I quit my comfortable job with no plan

April 21, 202616 minSolo episode
Listen on Spotify Apple Podcasts Watch on YouTube

From the outside, I had it figured out. I'd climbed fast in the corporate insurance world. I started at 21 with no degree and negotiated things most people my age never got: a company car, a home office, four weeks of vacation, a salary well above my level. On paper, the plan was working.

Underneath, I was burned out and depressed. My days were spent talking to people who lied about how hurt they were, and clients who lied about how responsible they were. I was winning on paper and losing in my soul. I'd been following the script: get the job, hit the marks, do the next thing you're supposed to do. I wasn't listening to myself.

Looking back, the signs were there for years. I couldn't stop questioning the waste. Filling out a form to fill out another form made no sense to me. I'd finish my work before 11am, then get handed the most complicated files as a reward, with the same targets as someone processing simple claims. So I stopped submitting my own performance reviews. My team thought I was crazy. My point was simple: you can't give me the hardest cases and hold me to everyone else's numbers. That refusal to accept a broken system is the same instinct that later built my business. In a big company it gets you labeled a problem. On your own, it's exactly the point.

The day it changed started like any other. I drove to the office, sat in the parking lot, and took one honest breath. That breath let everything in. What I thought was a heart attack turned out to be a panic attack. I sat there shaking and crying for about fifteen minutes, unable to open the door. My body forced the decision my mind had been avoiding.

I called my dad, my mom, and my wife. They said the same thing in different words: people like us don't get to quit on a whim. Line up another job, get the security, then leave. They were right on paper. They were wrong for me. So I walked in with no resignation letter and quit on the spot. My boss screamed, then came back later and apologized. It didn't change anything. I was out.

The runway was thin, and plenty of people were disappointed in me. But the moment I made the call, I could breathe again. That peace was the signal I'd been ignoring for years.

If you're the one who looks successful on paper and feels none of it, here's what I learned. The checkmarks are not the goal. The job, the title, the version of success that looks good to everyone else can become a cage when you follow the script without listening to yourself. You don't need a perfect plan to leave the wrong thing. You need to be honest about where you are, and willing to trust the part of you that already knows.

Key takeaways

  • Winning on paper doesn't mean the life is right for you.
  • Burnout is information. The panic attack forced a decision I'd avoided for years.
  • "People like us don't get to quit" is safe advice that can still be wrong for you.
  • The instinct to cut waste and simplify is a liability in a big company and an asset in your own business.
  • When a hard, risky choice brings peace instead of panic, that's your answer.
